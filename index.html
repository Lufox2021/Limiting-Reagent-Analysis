Limiting Reagent Analysis

What is a limiting reagent?

When two reactants are mixed, they are rarely present in exactly the ratio the balanced equation calls for. The reaction stops when one reactant is used up. That reactant is the limiting reagent (limiting reactant): it limits how much product can form. The other reactant is the excess reagent; some of it is left over.

An analogy: a sandwich needs 2 slices of bread and 1 slice of cheese. With 10 slices of bread and 4 slices of cheese the bread alone would make 5 sandwiches, but the cheese allows only 4. Cheese is limiting, and 2 slices of bread are left over. What matters is how many you have relative to how many each sandwich needs. In chemistry, "how many" means moles and "how many each needs" is the coefficient.

The procedure

  1. Balance the equation. The coefficients give the mole ratios.
  2. Find molar masses of the reactants and the product (sum of atomic masses).
  3. Convert grams to moles for each reactant: moles = mass ÷ molar mass.
  4. Use the mole ratio (coef product ÷ coef reactant) to find how many moles of product each reactant could make. The reactant that makes the least product is the limiting reagent.
  5. Excess remaining: mol excess consumed = mol LR × (coef excess ÷ coef LR); convert to grams; subtract from the grams given.
  6. Theoretical yield: mol product = mol LR × (coef product ÷ coef LR); grams = mol × molar mass.
  7. Percent yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the reactant with the smaller mass as limiting. Mass doesn't decide; the moles of product each reactant can make does.
  • Comparing moles but forgetting the coefficients.
  • Calculating the theoretical yield from the excess reagent.
  • Using the mole ratio upside down; write the units out so the unwanted ones cancel.
  • Multiplying a molar mass by the coefficient. Molar mass is always for one formula unit.
  • Computing percent yield as theoretical ÷ actual (gives more than 100%).
